What is RadMap?

Monte Carlo transport, without the scripting.

RadMap uses the radiation transport toolkit Geant4 behind a graphical interface, so you can calculate external dose to a person in your own environment in minutes rather than days.

Full Monte Carlo transport

RadMap uses the Geant4 toolkit as its calculation engine and follows every particle through your geometry, from the decay that emits it to the dose map that scores it.

Shielding, scatter and skyshine

RadMap follows every photon through your shields and through the walls, floors, air and ground around them. Shielding, scattered radiation and skyshine all come out of one calculation: there are no buildup factors to look up and no separate mode to switch on. The V&V report covers all three.

3D geometry builder

Build your environment from nested boxes, tubes and spheres – with adjustable opening angles for shielding containers and shells. Position and rotate each solid in the 3D view, and save any part as a reusable volume for other projects.

Materials database

Choose from a wide selection of predefined materials from the NIST database, or mix existing materials by mass fraction – a concrete with iron ore, for example. Define your own materials, elements and isotopes in the material editor, and import or export whole material libraries.

Radioactive sources

Attach a source to any solid and choose from a large number of radioactive isotopes. Give the activity in becquerels or curies – as a total, or per mass or volume of the solid. Optionally follow the full decay chain.

Dose quantities

Score effective dose for a person, ambient dose equivalent H*(10) to compare with survey meters, or air kerma for instrument calibration. The conversion coefficients come from internationally agreed ICRP tables, and every report lists the publications behind its numbers.

Bremsstrahlung from beta emitters

Optional electron transport follows the betas of high-energy emitters such as Sr-90/Y-90 or P-32 and scores the bremsstrahlung they produce in lead or tungsten shielding.

Results & PDF reports

Every dose map shows its dose rate with its statistical uncertainty. A PDF report records the dose rates, the sources, the publications behind the numbers and a picture of the 3D scene.

Verified and validated

Every release comes with a V&V report, generated from RadMap's tests. It shows each result next to its reference: published data, or measurements from shielding experiments. Projects shipped with RadMap let you check that your installation reproduces the released results. The report and the instructions for the check are in the documentation.

Workflow

From empty world to dose report in five steps.

Step 1 of 5

Build the environment

Model the environment as nested solids inside a world box. Every solid sits fully inside its parent, and solids may not overlap.

Shapes
Boxes, tubes and spheres – tubes and spheres can be hollow or cut open with angles
Placement
Position and rotation relative to the parent solid
Materials
Predefined materials from the NIST database, mixes of them, or your own materials, elements and isotopes
Reuse
Save any part as a volume and load it into other projects

Step 2 of 5

Place sources

Make any solid radioactive by attaching a source to it.

Isotopes
Radionuclides from Geant4's decay data – from calibration and industrial sources to rare nuclides
Activity
Total, or per mass or volume of the solid
Units
Becquerel (nBq – EBq) or curie (nCi – ECi)
Decay chain
Optionally follow the full chain of decay products

Step 3 of 5

Place dose maps

Place box-shaped dose maps where you need the dose rate. They score the photon fluence without disturbing the transport and may overlap solids.

Effective dose
Sv/h, for an AP, PA, ROT, ISO, LLAT or RLAT body orientation
H*(10)
Sv/h, for area monitoring and comparison with survey meters
Air kerma
Gy/h, for instrument calibration, such as ionisation chambers

Step 4 of 5

Simulate

Choose how many primary particles to simulate and start the run.

Precision
Ten times the particles take ten times as long and give about a third of the uncertainty
Free license
Up to 50,000 primary particles per run
Electrons
Optional transport for bremsstrahlung from beta emitters behind high-Z shielding

Step 5 of 5

Collect results

Each dose map shows its dose rate with its statistical uncertainty.

PDF report
Dose rates, sources and a picture of the 3D scene
Traceability
Every report lists the publications behind its numbers
Project lock
Inputs are locked while results are attached, so the two always match

OpenGL 3.3 or later – any graphics card or integrated graphics from the last decade; some virtual machines and remote desktop sessions lack it
Account
Administrator rights to install
How do I verify the download?

Compare the installer's SHA-256 checksum with the one shown under the Download button, which is also in RadMap_setup.exe.sha256 on the release page. In PowerShell, in the folder you downloaded to, run Get-FileHash .\RadMap_setup.exe -Algorithm SHA256 – the Hash it prints must match. A matching checksum shows that the installer arrived complete and unaltered.

What does RadMap cost?

Full licenses are not yet for sale; until they are, we offer a free 30-day trial. In RadMap, open Help → License and click Copy Machine ID, then send the ID to info@radmap.tech. When you receive your .lic file, open the same dialog, click Install License File… and select it. The license takes effect immediately, is tied to that machine and cannot be transferred. How long does a simulation take?

It depends on how many primary particles your case needs, and on your computer. As a guide, a typical geometry with 106 primary particles (requires a full license) finishes in about 30 seconds on a standard laptop, with a statistical uncertainty of calculated dose rates below 5 %. Run time grows linearly with the number of particles and drops with every extra CPU core, since simulations are multi-threaded. Enabling electron transport also makes a run noticeably slower.

A heavily shielded or distant dose map may need many times more particles than one in direct view of the source. Increase the number until the uncertainty reported with each dose rate is small enough for your purpose.

Which dose quantities can RadMap calculate?

Effective dose in Sv/h – the whole-body quantity radiation protection limits are expressed in – for six body orientations: AP, PA, ROT, ISO, LLAT and RLAT. ROT is generally the most meaningful choice for a single source; with several sources at different angles, prefer ROT or ISO. Ambient dose equivalent H*(10) in Sv/h is the operational quantity for area monitoring – what survey meters are calibrated to show. Air kerma in Gy/h is the reference quantity for calibrating instruments such as ionisation chambers, and for comparison with figures quoted in air kerma.

Where do the conversion coefficients come from?

RadMap transports particles and scores the fluence averaged over each dose map's volume. That fluence is multiplied by coefficients from internationally agreed tables – RadMap applies them, it does not derive them. Effective dose uses ICRP 116 (photons from 10 keV to 10 GeV); H*(10) and air kerma use ICRP 74 (10 keV to 10 MeV). Dose → Dose settings shows the publication for each quantity, and every PDF report lists the publications its numbers are based on.

When should I enable electron transport?

Only when your source is a high-energy beta emitter, such as Sr-90/Y-90 or P-32, in or near high-Z shielding like lead or tungsten. The electrons are then stopped in the shield and radiate bremsstrahlung photons that can add substantially to the dose outside it. For most sources the photons carry all the dose of interest, so the option is off by default – it noticeably increases simulation time.

Can I reuse geometry between projects?

Yes. Right-click a solid and choose Save Volume to save it, with everything inside it, as a .sol file – optionally with its sources and dose maps, and always with the materials it uses. Load Volume inserts it into another project. Material libraries can also be exported and imported from the Materials menu.

Can I simulate metastable isotopes such as Tc-99m?

Not directly. Sources use the decay scheme of the ground-state nucleus, so Tc-99 does not give the dose rate of Tc-99m. Cs-137 does give the expected result, because it decays to the metastable Ba-137m. The documentation's known limitations list this and what else RadMap does not calculate.
