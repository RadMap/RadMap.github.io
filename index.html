What is RadMap?

Radiation physics, without the scripting.

RadMap uses the radiation transport toolkit Geant4 behind a graphical interface, so you can calculate external dose to a person in a customised environment in minutes rather than days.

Full Monte Carlo transport

RadMap uses the Geant4 toolkit as its calculation engine and follows every particle through your geometry. Simulations are multi-threaded, so run time drops with every extra CPU core.

3D geometry builder

Build your environment from nested boxes, tubes and spheres – with adjustable opening angles for shielding containers and shells. Position and rotate each solid in the 3D view, and save any part as a reusable volume for other projects. RadMap checks for overlapping volumes before every run.

Materials database

Start from about 280 predefined materials – elements, compounds, concrete, lead glass and ICRP/ICRU tissues. Define your own materials, elements and isotopes in the material editor, and import or export whole libraries.

Radioactive sources

Attach a source to any solid and choose from nearly 3,000 radioactive isotopes. Give the activity in becquerels or curies – as a total, or per mass or volume of the source material, from which RadMap derives the total. Optionally follow the full decay chain.

Effective dose, H*(10) and air kerma

Score effective dose in Sv/h for six body orientations – AP, PA, ROT, ISO, LLAT and RLAT – ambient dose equivalent H*(10) in Sv/h for area monitoring, or air kerma in Gy/h. Dose maps don’t disturb the transport and may overlap solids.

Traceable conversion data

Fluence is converted to dose with coefficients from published tables – ICRP 116 for effective dose, ICRP 74 for H*(10) and air kerma. RadMap applies them, it doesn’t derive them, and every report lists the publications behind its numbers.

Bremsstrahlung from beta emitters

Optional electron transport follows the betas of high-energy emitters such as Sr-90/Y-90 or P-32 and scores the bremsstrahlung they produce in lead or tungsten shielding.

Results & PDF reports

Every dose map shows its dose rate with statistical uncertainty. Export a PDF report with the dose maps, sources, data sources and a picture of the 3D scene – the project locks so results always match their inputs.

How do I get a full license?

In RadMap, open Help → License. The dialog shows your machine ID and copies it to the clipboard – send it to info@radmap.tech. When you receive your .lic file, open the same dialog, click Install License File… and select it. The license takes effect immediately, is tied to that machine and cannot be transferred. If it has an expiry date, the same dialog shows it; when it expires, RadMap returns to the free license.

How many primary particles do I need?

It depends on the precision you need. Run time grows linearly with the number of particles, while the statistical uncertainty only shrinks with the square root: ten times more particles takes ten times longer and reduces the standard deviation by roughly a factor of three. The free license’s 50,000 particles per run are enough for most screening calculations.

Which dose quantities can RadMap calculate?

Effective dose in Sv/h – the whole-body quantity radiation protection limits are expressed in – for six body orientations: AP, PA, ROT, ISO, LLAT and RLAT. ROT is generally the most meaningful choice for a single source; with several sources at different angles, prefer ROT or ISO. Ambient dose equivalent H*(10) in Sv/h is the operational quantity for area monitoring – what survey meters are calibrated to show. Air kerma in Gy/h describes the radiation field itself with no body present, for comparison with figures quoted in air kerma.

Where do the conversion coefficients come from?

RadMap transports particles and scores the fluence through each dose map. That fluence is multiplied by coefficients from internationally agreed tables – RadMap applies them, it does not derive them. Effective dose uses ICRP 116 (photons from 10 keV to 10 GeV); H*(10) and air kerma use ICRP 74 (10 keV to 10 MeV). Dose → Dose settings shows the publication for each quantity, and every PDF report lists the publications its numbers are based on.

When should I enable electron transport?

Only when your source is a high-energy beta emitter, such as Sr-90/Y-90 or P-32, in or near high-Z shielding like lead or tungsten. The electrons are then stopped in the shield and radiate bremsstrahlung photons that can add substantially to the dose outside it. For most sources the photons carry all the dose of interest, so the option is off by default – it noticeably increases simulation time.

Can I reuse geometry between projects?

Yes. Right-click a solid and choose Save Volume to save it, with everything inside it, as a .sol file – optionally with its sources and dose maps, and always with the materials it uses. Load Volume inserts it into another project. Material libraries can also be exported and imported from the Materials menu.

Can I simulate metastable isotopes such as Tc-99m?

Not directly. Sources use the decay scheme of the ground-state nucleus, so Tc-99 does not give the dose rate of Tc-99m. Cs-137 does give the expected result, because it decays to the metastable Ba-137m.
