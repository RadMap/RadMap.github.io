What is RadMap?

Serious radiation physics, without the scripting.

RadMap uses the radiation transport toolkit Geant4 behind a graphical interface, so you can calculate external photon dose to a person in a customised environment in minutes rather than days.

Full Monte Carlo transport

Photon histories are tracked through your geometry using the Geant4 toolkit as calculation engine. Simulations are multi-threaded, so run time drops with every extra CPU core.

3D geometry builder

Build your environment from nested boxes and tubes. Position and rotate each solid relative to its parent and inspect everything in the 3D geometry view.

Materials database

Pick from hundreds of predefined materials – or define your own compounds and mixtures when you need something special.

Hundreds of isotopes

Assign any of several hundred radioactive isotopes to a solid, set its activity, and choose whether to simulate the full decay chain.

Dose maps with ICRP 116

Place dose maps wherever you need an answer. The registered flux is converted to dose with ICRP 116 coefficients for AP, PA, RLAT, LLAT, ROT or ISO exposure geometry.

Results & PDF reports

Browse dose rates with their statistical uncertainty and export a PDF report. Projects with attached results are locked, keeping inputs and outputs consistent.

Workflow

From empty world to dose report in five steps.

Step 1 of 5

Build the environment

Every project starts with a world box. Inside it you add sub-solids – boxes and tubes of any material – to model rooms, shielding walls, tanks and pipes. Volumes are nested and may not overlap.

  • File → New Project creates the world
  • Right-click → New sub solid adds a box or tube
  • Set dimensions, position, rotation and material

Step 2 of 5

Place sources

Decide which parts of the environment are radioactive. Attach a source to any solid to make it radioactive.

  • Choose element and isotope from the library
  • Set the activity in GBq
  • Tick Full chain to include decay products

Step 3 of 5

Place dose maps

Where do you need to know the dose rate? Place box-shaped dose maps there. They register the photon flux passing through and convert it to dose rate.

  • Position the dose map like any other solid
  • Pick the exposure geometry: AP PA RLAT LLAT ROT ISO

Step 4 of 5

Simulate

Want a quick estimate or a precise answer? Choose the number of primary particles – more particles take longer but give a smaller uncertainty – and hit Simulate.

  • Set the number of primary particles
  • The run uses all cores of your computer

Step 5 of 5

Collect results

Browse through the dose maps and read the dose rate for each one, presented with its statistical uncertainty. Then share the results as a report.

  • Report → Create PDF report
  • The project locks so results always match their inputs

How do I get a full license?

In RadMap, open Help → License and send the machine ID shown there to info@radmap.tech. You'll receive a .lic file that you install from the same dialog. Licenses are machine-specific and cannot be transferred.

Why is the installer so large?

To simulate radiation accurately, RadMap needs data on a large number of particle interactions, material properties and radioactive sources. This data takes up most of the space – the executables are only a small fraction of it.

Where is RadMap installed?

By default in C:\Program Files (x86)\RadMap. You can choose another folder during installation. Unpacking the Geant4 data takes a minute or two.

Will uninstalling remove my projects?

No. RadMap is removed through Control Panel → Programs, and your project files (.rma) and results are left untouched.

Can I simulate metastable isotopes such as Tc-99m?

Not currently. Sources are defined by their ground-state nucleus, so excited states cannot be specified directly.

Which dose quantity does RadMap report?

Dose maps register photon flux, which is converted to dose using ICRP 116 conversion coefficients for the selected exposure geometry. Note that the effective dose assumes whole-body exposure.
